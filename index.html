    Soil

    Make your own mix

    Almost all pre-bagged cactus soil is bad. “Cactus and succulent soil” is typically pure peat and bark with about 5% perlite mixed in. MiracleGro adds slow-release fertilizer on top of the peat. Mixing your own is the highest-leverage thing on this page.

    The lazy recipe

    65% pumice, 35% peat-free garden soil. That is the whole thing. If you do nothing else here, do this.

    The advanced mix

    Think of it in terms of organic and inorganic. The organic fraction is there to hold nutrients and water. The inorganic fraction is there to hold air. Anything that fails at one of those jobs does not belong in the mix.

    The organic portion

    • A high quality garden soil or top soil. Sift through 1/8″ mesh to remove large chunks and any bark.
    • Optionally enrich with earthworm castings at 1:3 to 1:4.
    • Further optional amendments: alfalfa meal, kelp, fish bone meal, insect frass. Compost the organic mix well before using it if you add these, and avoid high-nitrogen-only additions like manure or blood meal.
    • Not peat moss. It has strange water absorption properties: once it goes dry it turns hydrophobic and takes a long time to soak up water again. A surfactant in the water helps bring it back around.
    • Not coir, because of its low nutrient holding capacity. If the organic fraction is not good at holding nutrients or water, it is not doing its job.

    The inorganic portion

    • The goal is a durable, porous particle with medium-low density.
    • For a single ingredient: pumice, lava or scoria, expanded shale, or a hard-fired porous clay product.
    • Clay products by particle size: Turface (seedling), Espoma Soil Perfector (medium), Seramis, and LECA or clay pebbles (large).
    • Under 5% each, optional: granite from chicken grit or landscaping decomposed granite for micronutrients, basalt for the same, limerock or limestone or oyster shell for calcium, and zeolite to improve the nutrient holding capacity of the mix. Soak zeolite in nutrient solution before mixing it in.
    • Biochar is newer and shows a lot of promise: the nutrient holding of an organic with the water and drainage behavior of an inorganic. It must be pre-charged, or it soaks up everything you feed your plants for a while before it equalizes.
    • Sift the inorganics over window screen to remove fines, dust and sand. Washing or rinsing is optional but helps further.
    Leave out

    Vermiculite and perlite retain too much moisture, and perlite floats out of suspension in a mix. Sand causes compaction and reduces aeration and drainage.

    Particle size follows plant size
    1/8″seed sowing
    1/8–1/4″seedlings
    1/8–3/8″yearlings
    3/8″adults

    Increase the size of the inorganic particles at every up-pot as they mature. Avoid anything over 1/2″.

    How much inorganic

    The ratio depends on your climate, your watering frequency, the species and the pot type. Fifty-fifty in terracotta, unglazed clay or fabric is the starting point.

    Terracotta, clay or fabric50 / 50

    Push the inorganic share higher than that for glazed ceramic or plastic pots, for bridgesii specifically, and if you live somewhere very wet or humid.

    At potting

    You must have a drainage hole, and no saucer. Add a mycorrhizal fungi product such as Mykos at time of potting, and apply granular mycorrhizae directly to the roots at every up-pot after that.

    Buying it

    Buying soil and rocks online gets very expensive because of shipping. Scout locally: nurseries, garden centers, hydroponics stores, landscape supply yards and irrigation supply shops. As one real example, 3/8″ lava runs about $1.63/qt online against $0.26/qt locally. Buying the larger bags helps a lot too.

    Water

    Drench it, then let it go all the way dry

    For adult rooted plants: when the soil gets thoroughly dry all the way through, it is fine to water that day, or to let it sit dry for a few more days. That is the entire schedule.

    • When you water, drench the soil.
    • Submerging the pot up to the soil line until saturated is the preferred method. Top watering can be fine, and is required for larger pots.
    • Keep splashing wet soil up onto the column to a minimum. It can sometimes invite fungal issues.
    The temperature rule

    Keep watering as long as daily highs are 60°F or above and nightly lows are above 35°F. If temperatures are expected to drop below that range, let the soil go dry before it happens.

    Dormancy

    When temperatures are below 60°F and the soil is dry, they go dormant and stop growing. They can survive a dormant state like that for as long as a couple of years with no water. Doing nothing is genuinely the correct action.

    They can also survive below-freezing temperatures at night, as long as the soil is dry and the day temperatures go back above freezing. Placing a cup over the column tips to protect them from frost is not a bad idea.

    Rain

    If it rains many days in a row in your area, at any time of year, you will need to protect them with an awning, shelter, greenhouse or similar. Plants in the ground with well-draining soil around them are much less of a worry: they get better drainage and are more resilient overall.

    Feeding

    Without nutrients they get sick and die

    This is not optional and it is not a light touch. Most of these plants are badly underfed.

    What to use

    Masterblend three-part formula, which includes Epsom salts and calcium nitrate. Use as directed on the label. Amount and frequency depend on your exact soil composition and conditions. MegaCrop and MaxiGro are fine too.

    Measure it

    Get a cheap TDS meter (total dissolved solids). It probably has more than one mode: use EC, electrical conductivity, rather than PPM, parts per million. The PPM unit has multiple standards and more confusing conversions, so just avoid it.

    • Test your starting water. Aim for a low number here. If your starting water is over 0.35 you could probably use a water filter.
    • Top water the plant with regular water to about 20% runoff, collect the runoff, and test it.
    • Subtract the starting water value. What remains is your soil’s TDS.
    Soil EC, after subtracting your source water
    1.0 weak2.0 full3.0 strong4.0 toxic

    If you want to go over 2.0, work your way up gradually. Modify your feed schedule and strength to reach the runoff level you are aiming for.

    The regimen

    Bottom water with fertilizer solution at 2.5 to 3.0 EC on four out of every five waterings. On the fifth, flush by top watering with plain unfertilized water. If your soil mix is different, your schedule and strength could be quite different.

    The flush

    That fifth watering is a great time to use a beneficial soil microbe addition. Recharge helps the nutrients in the soil break down into a form the plant can use, and it also provides plant growth hormones, improves the nutrient retention of the soil, and prevents the soil from going hydrophobic.

    Keep checking your runoff TDS and pH occasionally, especially if you see anything funny going on, so you do not end up with nutrient salt buildup or a runaway pH.

    pH and lockout

    Balance your feed to 6.0–6.5, which is also the target soil pH. Test it with strips on collected runoff water. Use white vinegar to bring pH down and baking soda to bring it up.

    To check soil pH directly, mix some of the soil into a bit of distilled water to a mud-like consistency, wait about an hour, then test. If your soil pH is out of the target range your roots will not absorb some nutrients, which is known as lockout, and your plant’s health will decline.

    • Soil pH tends to drift toward feed pH, but soil components and nutrients shift it too.
    • Raises pH: lime, and too much nitrate nitrogen.
    • Lowers pH: some lava rock, and too much ammonia nitrogen.

    Foliar feeding

    Liquid kelp gives plant growth hormones and boosts growth. Maxicrop works well and Jones Juice is another highly recommended product. Mist the columns after dark; the plants absorb through their pores. Optionally add humic or fulvic acid, which acts synergistically with kelp to increase absorption.

    Humic and fulvic acids can also be used as a soil soak to break down clay elements in the soil, if you want to increase your soil’s water holding or nutrient holding capacity.

    You can also foliar feed a nutrient solution like Masterblend to cuttings that have no roots yet, if they are looking rough but cannot take up their own nutrients.

    Blue farina

    For cacti carrying a blue waxy farina layer, adding cocowet to the foliar feed helps it spread evenly, stay wet across the surface, increase absorption, and avoid damaging the farina. For the best farina results, avoid foliar feeding plants with strong blue layers altogether.

    Salt Lake water

    Adapted, not sourced: valley tap runs hard and alkaline and will often land above the 0.35 EC threshold on its own. Test yours before building a feed schedule around these numbers, and expect the flush to matter more here than it would on soft water.

    Light

    As much as they will take without burning

    That is the whole target. In areas and seasons where the sun is intense, some peak summer days run a little strong and they would probably appreciate shade from 2 to 5 p.m., but it is not required.

    • All light increases must be made gradually, or sunburn happens.
    • Sunburn looks like yellowing, either splotchy or at the tip and near the spines. It turns into permanent scarring.
    • Not enough light leads to skinny new growth, which is called etiolation.

    Indoors

    These plants do not do well indoors unless they are under a very strong grow light, or right next to a south-facing window that gets several hours of direct sun per day. If you have to grow adult plants indoors away from direct sunlight, you want a serious grow light, not a cheap one. Viparspectra is acceptable on the budget end; Mars Hydro and Spider Farmer above that.

    PPFD targets

    500 PPFD, a measure of how much usable light actually lands on the plant, gets you maintenance during near-dormancy temperatures, with some slight etiolation possible. For full healthy vigorous growth, aim for 1000.

    Utah

    Adapted, not sourced: Salt Lake sits above 4,200 ft, so the same clock hours carry more UV than they would nearer sea level. Treat that 2 to 5 p.m. shade cloth as closer to required than optional here, particularly for a plant that has just come out of a winter indoors.

    Winter

    The one rule Salt Lake breaks

    The source guidance is that these survive below-freezing nights as long as the soil is dry and day temperatures recover above freezing. A Salt Lake winter does not reliably offer that, so this section is adapted rather than followed.

    • The species is rated to roughly 20°F bone dry. The valley is USDA zone 7a to 7b, where annual lows reach 0 to 5°F. That gap is why the pot comes inside.
    • Use the temperature rule to decide when to stop watering: once nightly lows are heading below 35°F, let the soil go dry and leave it dry.
    • Then move it indoors before hard freezes settle in, generally through October.
    • Indoors it needs a very strong grow light or a south-facing window with several hours of direct sun. Anything less and it etiolates.
    • 500 PPFD is enough to hold it through dormancy. Below that, expect stretching.
    • A dormant, dry plant holds for a couple of years without water. There is nothing to do and nothing to fix.
    Cold plus wet

    Rot is caused by soil that is too wet for too long, or wet at too low a temperature. Anything left outside through a wet stretch needs an awning, shelter or greenhouse. A cup over the column tips is decent frost protection for a light freeze.

    Cuttings

    Cutting, callousing and rooting

    Sometimes you buy an unrooted cutting. Sometimes you want to cut off a column to propagate. Sometimes you have to cut because of rot or other disease. The handling is the same either way.

    1

    Cut

    STERILIZE THE BLADE BEFORE AND AFTER EVERY CUT

    Sterilize the knife with alcohol before and after each individual cut, to avoid spreading disease from one cut to the next.

    If you are cutting rot away, inspect the inside of the cut. If there are dark or discolored parts of the inner flesh, re-sterilize and cut more. Keep going until the face is clean.

    Immediately after cutting you can optionally apply powdered sulphur to the cut end to protect it from disease and rot, and/or powdered rooting hormone. If you bought the cutting already cut, do not worry about any of that.

    2

    Callous

    ABOUT TWO WEEKS. FASTER IN FRONT OF A FAN.

    Let cuttings callous by putting them somewhere shaded and dry with good airflow. Sitting in front of a fan can do the job quickly.

    After about two weeks there should be a light tan scab over the cut, and it is ready to try to root.

    3

    Root

    VERY LIGHT WATER. NEVER SOGGY.

    The idea is to tease roots out with some very light water exposure, without letting the cut end sit soggy or damp for long, which will rot it. There are a few ways to do it:

    One. Put it cut-end down in some porous inorganic and water it very lightly every so often.

    Two. Set the cut on some rich soil and stake the cutting upright. You would not water much using this method.

    Three. Plant it straight into a permanent pot and soil mix and water it extremely sparingly for a long time.

    Do not bury the cut too deep. The more green flesh below the surface, the higher the chance of rot.

    4

    Watch for it to plump

    THE SIGN THAT ROOTS ARE DRINKING

    With the third method the tell is this: when you give it a small amount of water and you can see the column plump a little, its roots are taking up water. From there you can move onto a normal watering schedule.

    If a rootless cutting is looking rough in the meantime, you can foliar feed it a nutrient solution such as Masterblend. It cannot take anything up through roots it does not have yet.

    From seed

    Starting from scratch

    This protocol comes from thousands of seeds sown across 61 varieties and five seasons, one batch each fall, with the losses that taught it.

    The short version

    Sterilize everything, sow on the surface of damp vermiculite, seal the container, hold it at 80°F out of direct sun, and do not open it for months.

    1

    Store the seed

    MONTHS ON A SHELF. YEARS IN THE FRIDGE.

    Seeds are fine in an envelope or baggie on a dark shelf for months, but eventually the germination rate gradually declines.

    To hold viability for a year or three, you want lower humidity and lower temperature: transfer to small paper envelopes, put those in a doubled-up Ziploc with a quality desiccant pack, and keep it in the fridge.

    2

    Sterilize everything

    180°F INTERNAL FOR 30 MINUTES

    Seeds and seedlings need elevated humidity. You can mist three or four times a day around the clock for the first couple of months, or you can seal them up and not water at all for months. Clear plastic wrap holds humidity better than lids. To seal them, sterilization is mandatory, or fungus gnat eggs, algae or mold take over.

    Vermiculite and perlite are sterile. Pumice and turface can be boiled. Soil needs to reach 180°F for 30 minutes internally, checked with a meat thermometer, or 220°F instantaneously. In the oven, about 30 minutes at 300–350°F works. In the microwave, moisten the soil, use freezer bags cracked open to vent, and run it 3 minutes, then check the internal temperature.

    The other common source of contamination is on the seed casing. A diluted hydrogen peroxide bath does wonders for this, and also oxygenates and hydrates the seeds, improving germination times and rates. Pure 3% H₂O₂ for an hour will sterilize. Six hours at 50:50 3% H₂O₂ to distilled water, or 1:10 for 24 hours, does more for germination while keeping the sterilizing benefit.

    While sowing, keep work surfaces wiped clean and wash hands and tools too.

    3

    100% vermiculite

    SIFTED IN TWO GRADES

    Earlier attempts using peat and perlite brought fungus gnats and water retention problems. A later mix of pumice, turface and sterilized composted soil sifted through 1/8″ window screen, topped with fine vermiculite, gave better results but still had issues.

    After proving it out, the strong advice is pure vermiculite. Sift through 1/8″ mesh for the main portion, then sift an extra fine layer through window screen to make a top layer roughly 3/16″ deep. It eliminates the chance of most pests, fungus and algae, and appears to have no downsides.

    Moisture level before sowing is critical: squeeze a fistful hard and only a few drops should come out. Vermiculite carries no nutrients of its own, so fertilization begins as soon as you uncover.

    4

    Containers and sowing

    ON THE SURFACE. LIGHT IS REQUIRED.

    The particular takeout containers matter. Look for clear containers of sturdy plastic, not thin and flimsy, at least 4″ deep to allow enough growth before up-potting, and cubic to maximize space on the heat mat. You want something you can puncture holes in the bottom of later, once the containers are unsealed.

    To sow: pour the distilled water through a paper towel draped over a cup to catch the seeds, dab them dry, brush them onto a paper plate, then transfer to a folded paper to use as a sowing tool.

    Sow 3/8″ apart, which comes out to four to six seeds per square inch. Seeds sown too closely together slow significantly. Sow directly on top of the media and do not top dress or push them down. Make sure the top layer is fine enough that seeds do not fall into cracks. Mist over the top with purified or distilled water, then wrap well with plastic wrap.

    5

    Heat and shade

    68–86°F. 80°F CONSTANT.

    Set the heat mat on a foam insulation board to direct the heat up. Run 80°F constant during germination, then after a few weeks move to 80°F day and 60°F night using a thermostat with day and night settings. Install the temperature probe in a dummy container of soil kept at the same moisture as the others, through a hole poked in the side and sealed with silicone, rather than reading air. If you are germinating indoors where temps are already 68–86°F, you do not need the mat after germination.

    Keep sealed containers out of direct sunlight. They are mini greenhouses and just an hour in sun can cook them and kill all the seeds. Indirect sun is fine, great even. Think of it in terms of shadow: in a shadow is indirect, casting a shadow is direct. Even some paper laid over them that diffuses light through is enough. You can germinate with bright indirect sun and no artificial light at all.

    6

    Uncover, feed, ramp the light

    1.5 TO 6 MONTHS. TWO WEEKS TO UNCOVER.

    Check on your seeds at minimum every couple of days, to catch light or temperature problems and contamination early. Somewhere between 1.5 months after germination, when they have at least a few rows of spines, and 6 months, it is time to acclimate them to ambient humidity.

    Poke holes in the plastic wrap, expand the holes, then remove the wrap over the course of about two weeks. Mist a few times a day immediately after removing it, tapering to once a day and then not at all. Poke drainage holes in the bottom of the containers at this point.

    Introduce fertilizer slowly and gradually. 0.7 EC is a good starting point, and they can be ramped up to full strength fairly quickly.

    Let the soil get on the drier side without ever going bone dry. These do not have much ability to hold water and will get stressed and stunted if they go too dry. When misting, the soil can go dry below the surface and be hard to notice, so bottom watering on occasion is good practice.

    • Start at 12 hours on and 12 off, and increase the day length once the light is as close as you are comfortable with.
    • Light should be ramped up continuously and very gradually. Moving the fixture closer is one way to do it. Keep pushing until you see light stress, then back off, let them recover, and push again.
    • They should be able to take full sun by the time they are an inch and a half tall, which is roughly a tenfold increase from germination levels.
    • Surround the growing area with textured mylar, or matte white like spray-painted cardboard or butcher paper. Measured with a light meter, this doubled the light the plants received.
    • Barrina T5 LEDs are sufficient for germination, but within a few months you would need to move up to a dimmable panel.
    • When spring temperatures make the growing space too warm, move them outside. Start under multiple layers of shade cloth and acclimate to full sun over about a month.
    • Foliar feed with liquid kelp, occasionally water in Recharge, and use granular mycorrhizae applied to the roots at up-pot. And label everything.
    What goes wrong

    Seedlings have been lost to sunburn, dehydration, poor soil mix, overwatering, damping off and rust fungus. Thrips and fungus gnats have had to be fought off. Trays have been tipped and dumped. Cats have pissed in the soil and killed them. Check on them every couple of days.

    Troubleshooting

    Squishy, or just brown?

    Rot is a discolored spot that gives when you press it. Brown that stays hard is not rot. That single test sorts most panics before you reach for a knife.

    Firm, dry, brown

    It does not give under a press. Brown, hard tissue working evenly up from the base is a column aging normally, and it is called corking. Nothing to do. It does not reverse and it is not supposed to.

    Discolored and squishy

    It gives under a light press. Caused by soil that stayed too wet for too long, or was wet at too low a temperature. Cut it today, and see the first entry below for how.

    Discolored spot, squishy

    Cause: rot. Too wet for too long, or at too low a temperature.

    Treatment: cut the rot out with a sharp knife. Disinfect the knife with alcohol before each individual cut. Keep cutting until no discoloration is visible on the inner flesh. Dust the cuts with sulphur powder and let them dry. Then reduce moisture.

    Red all the way around, from the base going up

    Cause: phosphorus or potassium deficiency. The tell is that the tip is not red, or is less red than the rest.

    Treatment: use a well balanced fertilizer.

    Yellowish through the lower column, all the way around

    Cause: nitrogen and/or calcium deficiency.

    Treatment: use a well balanced fertilizer.

    Yellow or red on the ribs, tips and areoles, facing the sun

    Cause: sunburn. The giveaway is that it is focused on the protruding portions of the ribs, the tips, and around the areoles, on the side facing the sun.

    Treatment: provide shade for a few days. Burned areas may scar. Make light reintroductions gradually.

    White fuzzy clusters around areoles and between ribs

    Cause: mealybugs.

    Treatment: isolate affected plants. Remove and discard the soil. Scrub the mealies away with a toothbrush and alcohol.

    Small white or tan scab-looking spots with a darker center

    Possible cause: scale insects.

    Treatment: scrub with a toothbrush and alcohol. If they dislodge easily it was likely scale. If they do not dislodge easily, it could be scarring or a fungal issue instead.

    Scarring or small chunks missing from the tip

    Cause: the tip nibbler class of pests. Grasshoppers, slugs, snails.

    Treatment: use a ring of copper sheeting around the outside of the pots, since slugs and snails do not like copper. Foliar feed a mixture of gypsum and/or Epsom salts, which leaves a powder residue that tip nibblers dislike. Check under the pots for slugs and snails.

    Small discolored specks on the column

    Cause: the sucker class of pest insects. Slow-moving speck-like insects that do not jump, with no flying insects present, could be sucker mites. If the insects are oblong and flying insects are also present, it could be thrips larvae. Aphids are another candidate. Use yellow sticky traps to help detect the presence of thrips.

    Treatment: Spinosad spray concentrate (Monterey Garden Insect Spray), or alternatively Flying Skull Nuke Em. These are food safe sprays that coat the plant and kill anything that tries to suck juice from the column. Apply every 5 to 7 days for a minimum of 3 applications, to disrupt the egg, larvae and adult life cycle. Only apply at dusk, or you risk wet droplets of spray magnifying light and causing small black burn marks on the column.

    Tiny specks in the soil that jump when you blow on them

    Cause: springtails. Fast-moving speck-like insects on the cactus that do not fly are predator mites. Also in this group: worms, spiders, lacewings, ladybugs, praying mantis.

    Treatment: none. These are signs of a healthy ecosystem. Leave them be. They will not harm the plant.

    Glossary

    Every acronym on this page

    The measurements first, then the words, then the products that get named by brand.

    Units and acronyms

    PPFDPhotosynthetic Photon Flux Density

    How much usable light actually lands on the plant each second, counting only the wavelengths it can photosynthesize with. That is why it beats watts (which measures the bulb, not the plant) and lux (which is weighted for human eyes). For scale: a heavy overcast day is roughly 100–200, and full summer sun at midday is around 1500–2000. This guide asks for 500 to hold a plant steady and 1000 for real growth.

    ECElectrical Conductivity

    How well water carries an electric current, which goes up as more mineral salt dissolves in it. Fertilizer is dissolved salt, so EC is a direct read on how strong a feed is. Measured in millisiemens per centimeter. On the scale used here: 1.0 is weak, 2.0 is full strength, 3.0 is strong, 4.0 starts poisoning the plant.

    TDSTotal Dissolved Solids

    Everything dissolved in the water, and effectively the same underlying measurement as EC. The cheap handheld meter you buy is sold as a “TDS meter” and will have both modes on it.

    PPMParts Per Million

    The other mode on that meter, and the one to ignore. There are several competing PPM scales (500, 650, 700) and a number quoted on one means something different on another. EC has no such problem.

    pHAcidity or alkalinity

    A 0 to 14 scale. 7 is neutral, lower is acidic, higher is alkaline. Roots can only take up certain nutrients inside a narrow band, which is why the target here is 6.0–6.5 and why drifting outside it causes lockout, where the food is present in the soil but the plant cannot reach it.

    N-P-KNitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium

    The standard shorthand for what is in a fertilizer, often extended with Ca (calcium) and Mg (magnesium). Roughly: nitrogen drives green growth, phosphorus drives roots and flowering, potassium drives general vigor. Three-part formulas exist so you can set the ratio yourself instead of accepting a pre-blend.

    H₂O₂Hydrogen peroxide

    The drugstore bottle is 3% strength, and every dilution in the seed section is measured against that 3%. Used to sterilize the seed coat and to oxygenate and hydrate the seed before sowing.

    LECALightweight Expanded Clay Aggregate

    Fired clay pebbles, usually sold as clay balls or clay pebbles. One of the large-particle inorganic options for an adult mix.

    T5HOT5 High Output

    A fluorescent tube format. “T5” is the tube diameter, 5/8 of an inch; “High Output” is the brighter version. The two-foot, two-bulb fixture in the seed section is one of these.

    USDA zonePlant hardiness zone

    A map of average annual minimum winter temperature. Salt Lake Valley is 7a to 7b, meaning typical winter lows of 0 to 10°F. This species is good to about 20°F when bone dry, which is zone 9a. That gap is the entire reason the pot comes indoors.

    UVUltraviolet

    The part of sunlight that does the burning. It gets stronger with altitude, which is why 4,300 ft matters for a plant that has spent the winter inside.

    Words used here

    Areole

    The small raised cushion on a cactus where spines, branches and flowers all emerge. Unique to cacti, and the first place pests hide.

    Etiolation

    Stretched, skinny, pale growth caused by too little light. Permanent: the thin section never thickens back up, though healthy growth can resume above it.

    Callous

    The hard, dry, light tan scab that forms over a cut. A cutting is not ready to root until it has one, and skipping this is the usual reason cuttings rot.

    Corking

    Hard, brown, bark-like tissue forming at the base of an aging column. Completely normal. The test is that it stays firm, whereas rot gives under a press.

    Farina

    The powdery blue-white waxy bloom on the skin of some cacti. It rubs off permanently and foliar spray can mark it, which is why some growers skip foliar feeding entirely on their bluest plants.

    Lockout

    Soil pH drifts out of range, and roots stop absorbing whole groups of nutrients even though they are sitting right there. It looks exactly like a deficiency, so people feed more and make it worse.

    Damping off

    A fungal disease that collapses seedlings at the soil line. Caused by too much moisture and too little airflow, and it moves fast.

    Hydrophobic

    Soil that has dried so completely it now repels water instead of soaking it up. Peat is the usual offender. A surfactant brings it back.

    Surfactant

    Also called a wetting agent. Lowers water’s surface tension so it spreads and penetrates instead of beading up and rolling off.

    Runoff

    The water that drains out of the bottom of the pot after watering. Testing it is how you find out what is actually in your soil, rather than what you meant to put there.

    Flush

    Watering with plain, unfertilized water so it runs right through, washing accumulated fertilizer salt out of the soil.

    Foliar feeding

    Spraying nutrients onto the plant body rather than into the soil, absorbed through the surface. Done after dark.

    Up-pot

    Moving a plant into a larger pot, and with it a coarser mix than it had before.

    Air pruning

    What a fabric pot does. Roots reach the wall, hit dry air, and stop rather than circling the pot, so the plant branches into a denser root system instead.

    Mycorrhizae

    Beneficial fungi that colonize roots and effectively extend their reach for water and nutrients. Sold as granules, applied directly to the roots at potting.

    Substrate

    Whatever the plant is rooted in. Deliberately not the word “soil,” because for seedlings it is pure vermiculite and no soil at all.

    Frass

    Insect droppings, sold as an organic soil amendment.

    Dormancy

    The state a cactus drops into below 60°F with dry soil. Growth stops completely, and it can hold there for as long as a couple of years without water.

    Products named by brand

    FertilizerMasterblend, MegaCrop, MaxiGro

    Masterblend is a three-part formula, meaning you buy the base, calcium nitrate and Epsom salts separately and mix them, which is what gives you control over the ratio. The other two are single-bag alternatives and are fine.

    KelpMaxicrop, Jones Juice

    Liquid seaweed extracts, used as a foliar spray for the plant growth hormones rather than for nutrition.

    MicrobesRecharge

    A beneficial soil microbe product, added on the flush watering. Helps break nutrients into a usable form and keeps soil from going hydrophobic.

    Root fungiMykos

    A granular mycorrhizal inoculant, applied to the roots at potting and at every up-pot.

    Wetting agentCocowet

    Added to foliar spray so it spreads evenly across a waxy surface instead of beading, which protects the farina.

    Fired clayTurface, Espoma Soil Perfector, Seramis, LECA

    The same class of inorganic particle at four sizes, smallest to largest. Turface is seedling grade, Soil Perfector medium, LECA the large pebbles.

    Pest sprayMonterey Garden Insect Spray, Flying Skull Nuke Em

    The first is spinosad, a bacterially derived insecticide. Both are food safe and both target the sucking pests. Apply at dusk only.

    Grow lightsViparspectra, Mars Hydro, Spider Farmer, Barrina

    Viparspectra is the acceptable budget option; Mars Hydro and Spider Farmer sit above it. Barrina T5 LEDs are enough for germination, but within a few months seedlings need a dimmable panel.
